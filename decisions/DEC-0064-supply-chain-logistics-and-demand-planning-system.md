# DEC-0064 — Supply Chain, Logistics & Demand Planning System

**Decision ID:** DEC-0064  
**Title:** Establish the KAMLESH & SONS Supply Chain, Logistics and Demand Planning System  
**Status:** Accepted  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Decision Date:** 2026-08-08  
**Owner:** KAMLESH & SONS

---

## Decision

KAMLESH & SONS will manage supply, logistics and demand planning as an integrated system designed to maintain product availability, protect working capital, reduce avoidable cost and provide dependable customer service.

> **Put the right product in the right place at the right time, at the right economic cost.**

## 1. Supply Chain Principles

The system will prioritize availability, reliability, cash efficiency, quality, resilience and transparent supplier performance.

## 2. Demand Planning

Demand planning should combine historical sales, seasonality, promotions, product lifecycle, market signals and known business events.

## 3. Forecast Ownership

Material forecasts should have an accountable owner and documented assumptions.

## 4. Forecast Horizon

Planning should use appropriate horizons for operational, purchasing and strategic decisions.

## 5. Forecast Uncertainty

Where uncertainty is material, forecasts should show ranges or scenarios rather than false precision.

## 6. Demand Signals

Planning should incorporate:

- Sales history
- Current orders
- Promotions
- Seasonality
- New launches
- Stockouts
- Market events

## 7. Stockout Distortion

Historical demand should be adjusted where prior stockouts prevented actual sales from reflecting true demand.

## 8. Promotion Planning

Marketing promotions should be coordinated with demand planning and supplier capacity.

## 9. New Product Forecasting

New products should use analogous products, market evidence and pilot data where historical demand is unavailable.

## 10. Product Lifecycle

Planning should account for introduction, growth, maturity and decline.

## 11. Inventory Policy

Inventory targets should reflect demand, lead time, variability, margin, service requirements and cash constraints.

## 12. Safety Stock

Safety stock should be based on demand and supply uncertainty rather than arbitrary buffers.

## 13. Reorder Points

Reorder points should reflect expected demand during replenishment lead time plus appropriate safety stock.

## 14. Service Levels

Different products may have different service-level targets based on customer importance and economics.

## 15. ABC Analysis

Inventory may be prioritized using value, volume and business criticality.

## 16. Inventory Aging

Aging inventory should be visible and assigned for action.

## 17. Excess Inventory

Excess stock should trigger actions such as promotion, transfer, supplier negotiation or controlled liquidation.

## 18. Slow Movers

Slow-moving products should be reviewed for continued assortment fit and replenishment policy.

## 19. Dead Stock

Dead stock should be identified separately from healthy safety inventory.

## 20. Inventory Accuracy

Physical and system inventory should be reconciled through appropriate cycle counts and controls.

## 21. Warehouse Operations

Warehouses should use defined receiving, storage, picking, packing and dispatch processes.

## 22. Receiving

Inbound goods should be checked for quantity, condition, identity and required documentation.

## 23. Quality at Receipt

Damaged, incorrect or suspicious goods should be quarantined and resolved before normal availability.

## 24. Put-Away

Products should be stored in locations that support efficient retrieval, safety and inventory accuracy.

## 25. Picking

Picking processes should minimize errors and unnecessary movement.

## 26. Packing

Packing should protect products and meet customer and carrier requirements.

## 27. Dispatch

Dispatch should capture required order, carrier and tracking information.

## 28. Logistics Partners

Carriers should be evaluated on cost, reliability, coverage, damage and service quality.

## 29. Freight Economics

Transportation decisions should consider total delivered cost, not freight price alone.

## 30. Route Planning

Where practical, route planning should reduce delivery time, distance and cost while maintaining service commitments.

## 31. Delivery Tracking

Material shipments should have appropriate status visibility.

## 32. Proof of Delivery

Delivery confirmation should be retained where required for commercial or customer-service purposes.

## 33. Failed Deliveries

Failed deliveries should have clear reason codes and resolution workflows.

## 34. Returns Logistics

Returns should connect to customer-service, inventory, finance and supplier processes.

## 35. Reverse Logistics

Returned products should be classified for resale, repair, warranty, supplier return, recycling or disposal.

## 36. Supplier Lead Times

Supplier lead times should be measured from actual performance rather than relying solely on stated values.

## 37. Lead-Time Variability

Variation in supplier lead time should inform safety-stock and sourcing decisions.

## 38. Supplier Reliability

Supplier performance should include fill rate, quality, delivery accuracy and responsiveness.

## 39. Purchase Planning

Purchase orders should be based on demand, inventory position, open orders, lead time and cash constraints.

## 40. Purchase Order Discipline

Purchasing commitments should follow approved authority and procurement controls under DEC-0052.

## 41. MOQ Management

Minimum-order quantities should be evaluated against demand, margin, shelf life and cash impact.

## 42. Price Breaks

Volume discounts should be evaluated against carrying cost and demand risk.

## 43. Supplier Consolidation

Supplier consolidation may be used where it improves economics and reliability without creating excessive concentration risk.

## 44. Dual Sourcing

Critical products may require multiple sources when justified by risk and economics.

## 45. Supply Risk

Material supply risks should connect to the enterprise risk register under DEC-0051.

## 46. Critical Products

Products with high customer impact or limited substitutes should receive stronger availability planning.

## 47. Business Continuity

Critical supply chains should have contingency plans for major disruption.

## 48. Disruption Scenarios

Planning should consider:

- Supplier failure
- Transport disruption
- Customs delays
- Natural events
- Demand spikes
- Technology outages

## 49. Import Planning

Cross-border purchases should account for customs, duties, documentation and realistic transit times.

## 50. Landed Cost

Product economics should consider purchase price plus relevant freight, duties, handling and other acquisition costs.

## 51. Customs Documentation

Required import/export documents should be reconciled and retained under DEC-0058.

## 52. Product Compliance

Imported products should satisfy applicable product and labeling requirements before sale.

## 53. Supplier Quality

Quality expectations should be defined for material suppliers and products.

## 54. Quality Incidents

Material supplier quality incidents should trigger containment, investigation and corrective action.

## 55. Supplier Corrective Action

Repeated defects should lead to documented supplier corrective-action processes.

## 56. Supplier Scorecards

Material suppliers should be evaluated using consistent measures.

## 57. Procurement Coordination

Supply planning should integrate with procurement, finance, inventory and merchandising decisions.

## 58. Sales Coordination

Demand planning should incorporate sales pipeline and known customer commitments where relevant.

## 59. Marketing Coordination

Campaign plans should be checked against inventory and supplier capacity before launch.

## 60. Store Coordination

Store replenishment should use agreed service levels and inventory targets.

## 61. E-Commerce Coordination

Online availability should reflect reliable inventory information and fulfillment capacity.

## 62. Allocation

When supply is constrained, allocation should use explicit business rules rather than ad hoc decisions.

## 63. Backorders

Backorders should have customer communication, expected timing and ownership.

## 64. Available-to-Promise

Where feasible, customer promises should use realistic available-to-promise calculations.

## 65. Inventory Transfers

Transfers between locations should be justified by demand, availability and transfer cost.

## 66. Transfer Accuracy

Inter-location transfers should preserve inventory traceability.

## 67. Cycle Counting

Inventory counting frequency should reflect value, movement and risk.

## 68. Inventory Adjustments

Material adjustments should have documented reasons and appropriate approval.

## 69. Shrinkage

Inventory loss should be measured, investigated and connected to operational controls.

## 70. Warehouse Safety

Logistics operations must follow appropriate workplace safety requirements under DEC-0056 and DEC-0058.

## 71. Packaging Standards

Packaging should balance protection, cost, customer experience and environmental considerations.

## 72. Sustainability

Where commercially practical, logistics should reduce unnecessary waste, mileage and packaging.

## 73. Logistics Cost Analytics

Management should monitor transportation and fulfillment cost by relevant product, channel or order type.

## 74. Fulfillment SLA

Material fulfillment processes should have measurable service expectations.

## 75. Order Cycle Time

Order-to-dispatch and order-to-delivery times should be measured where useful.

## 76. Logistics Exceptions

Late, damaged, missing or misrouted shipments should enter exception workflows.

## 77. Carrier Claims

Material carrier claims should be documented and reconciled.

## 78. Demand Review

Forecasts should be reviewed periodically against actual demand and changed assumptions.

## 79. Forecast Accuracy

Forecast accuracy should be measured using appropriate metrics and interpreted by product class.

## 80. Forecast Bias

Persistent over- or under-forecasting should be investigated and corrected.

## 81. Scenario Planning

High-uncertainty periods should use alternative demand and supply scenarios.

## 82. Capacity Planning

Supplier, warehouse and transport capacity should be considered when planning growth.

## 83. Peak Planning

Peak seasons and major campaigns should receive advance inventory and logistics planning.

## 84. Replenishment Automation

Routine replenishment may be automated when data quality and business rules are sufficiently reliable.

## 85. AI in Supply Chain

AI may assist with demand forecasting, anomaly detection, replenishment recommendations and logistics optimization under DEC-0063.

## 86. Human Review

Material supply decisions should retain appropriate human review, especially during unusual conditions.

## 87. Data Integration

Supply-chain analytics should align with DEC-0060 and reliable source systems.

## 88. Technology Integration

Supply-chain systems should align with DEC-0061 and security requirements under DEC-0062.

## 89. Supplier Collaboration

Strategic suppliers may receive forecasts, inventory signals or performance information through controlled processes.

## 90. Confidentiality

Supplier and commercial information should be protected appropriately.

## 91. Supply Chain Dashboard

Management should eventually monitor:

- Forecast accuracy
- Service level
- Stockouts
- Inventory turns
- Aging
- Supplier performance
- Logistics cost
- Lead-time reliability

## 92. Working Capital

Supply-chain decisions should explicitly consider their effect on cash and working capital under DEC-0057.

## 93. Margin Protection

Supply decisions should consider gross-margin impact, not only availability.

## 94. Assortment Discipline

Products should remain in the assortment when customer value and economics justify their supply-chain burden.

## 95. Exit Decisions

Products with persistent poor demand, margin or operational burden should be reviewed for discontinuation.

## 96. Continuous Improvement

Supply-chain processes should improve through measured performance, root-cause analysis and supplier collaboration.

## 97. Key Risks

Major supply-chain risks include:

- Stockouts
- Excess inventory
- Forecast error
- Supplier failure
- Quality failures
- Logistics disruption
- Customs delays
- Inventory inaccuracies
- Working-capital pressure
- Single-source dependency

## 98. Governance

Material supply-chain risks and performance issues should be included in management and enterprise risk reviews.

## 99. Decision Quality

Supply decisions should make assumptions, trade-offs and constraints visible.

## 100. Success Standard

The supply-chain system is successful when leadership can answer:

- What will customers need?
- What do we have?
- What is coming?
- What is at risk?
- What should we buy?
- Where should it go?
- What will it cost?
- How will it affect cash and service?

## Implementation Sequence

1. Establish product, supplier and inventory master-data discipline.
2. Build demand forecasts and inventory policies by product class.
3. Measure supplier lead time, quality and fulfillment reliability.
4. Standardize warehouse, replenishment and transfer processes.
5. Integrate marketing, sales, procurement, inventory and logistics planning.
6. Build supply-chain dashboards and exception alerts.
7. Introduce scenario planning and controlled replenishment automation.
8. Add AI-assisted forecasting and optimization where data quality supports it.
9. Build disruption and continuity playbooks for critical supply chains.
