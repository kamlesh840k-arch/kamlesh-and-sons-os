# DEC-0063 — AI Governance, Automation & Intelligent Operations System

**Decision ID:** DEC-0063  
**Title:** Establish the KAMLESH & SONS AI Governance, Automation and Intelligent Operations System  
**Status:** Accepted  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Decision Date:** 2026-08-08  
**Owner:** KAMLESH & SONS

---

## Decision

KAMLESH & SONS will adopt AI and automation as controlled business capabilities, not as uncontrolled substitutes for accountability. Every material AI workflow must have an owner, defined purpose, appropriate data boundaries, measurable outcomes and human escalation.

> **Automate the repeatable, augment the intelligent, and keep accountability human.**

## 1. AI Principles

AI use will prioritize customer value, employee effectiveness, accuracy, security, privacy, transparency and measurable business benefit.

## 2. AI Inventory

The company should maintain an inventory of material AI systems, agents, automations and externally provided AI services.

## 3. AI Ownership

Each material AI workflow must have a business owner and a technical or operational owner where appropriate.

## 4. Approved Use Cases

AI may assist with drafting, search, summarization, forecasting, classification, customer support, analytics, knowledge retrieval and workflow automation where appropriate.

## 5. Prohibited or Restricted Uses

AI must not independently make high-impact legal, employment, financial, safety or customer decisions where human judgment or professional review is required.

## 6. Human-in-the-Loop

Material decisions must have a defined human review or escalation path.

## 7. AI Data Governance

Sensitive, confidential or personal information may only be processed by approved systems consistent with DEC-0055.

## 8. Prompt and Instruction Security

AI workflows should protect system instructions, credentials, confidential information and other sensitive context from unauthorized disclosure.

## 9. Output Validation

AI-generated content must be checked for material factual, legal, financial, operational and brand risks before external use.

## 10. Hallucination Controls

Important AI outputs should be grounded in authoritative business sources and verified before reliance.

## 11. Automation Standards

Automations should be documented with triggers, actions, permissions, failure modes and owners.

## 12. Least Privilege

AI agents and automations should receive only the permissions necessary to perform their tasks.

## 13. Approval Gates

Actions involving money, contracts, deletion, external communications, privileged access or material customer impact should require appropriate approval.

## 14. Auditability

Material automated actions should generate sufficient logs to understand what happened, when, and under which workflow.

## 15. Exception Handling

Every important automation should define what happens when inputs are missing, invalid, ambiguous or outside expected conditions.

## 16. Fail-Safe Behavior

Where uncertainty or system failure creates material risk, automation should fail safely and escalate rather than guessing.

## 17. AI Evaluation

Before material deployment, AI systems should be evaluated for accuracy, reliability, security, privacy, bias and operational usefulness.

## 18. Model Monitoring

Material AI systems should be monitored for performance degradation, drift, unexpected behavior and changed business conditions.

## 19. Vendor AI

Third-party AI providers should be assessed for security, privacy, data use, availability, pricing and exit considerations.

## 20. AI Contracts

Material AI services should have appropriate contractual controls covering data, confidentiality, security, service levels and intellectual property.

## 21. Intellectual Property

AI-generated or AI-assisted work should be reviewed for ownership, licensing and third-party rights as appropriate.

## 22. Customer Transparency

Where appropriate, customers should be informed when they are materially interacting with automated or AI-supported systems.

## 23. Employee Transparency

Employees should understand material AI systems that affect their work, responsibilities or performance.

## 24. AI Training

Relevant employees should receive practical training in responsible AI use, verification and data handling.

## 25. Automation Backlog

Potential automations should be prioritized by frequency, business impact, error reduction and implementation risk.

## 26. Process Before Automation

Broken or unclear processes should be simplified before being heavily automated.

## 27. Reversible Automation

New automations should be designed to be paused, rolled back or disabled when practical.

## 28. Operational Agents

AI agents may coordinate repetitive operational tasks but must operate within explicit permissions and escalation boundaries.

## 29. Customer Service AI

AI may support customer-service agents with search, summarization and response drafting while preserving human ownership of sensitive cases.

## 30. Knowledge Assistant

The company may provide controlled internal AI search over approved policies, procedures and business documentation.

## 31. Finance AI

AI may assist with reconciliation, anomaly detection and reporting, but material accounting judgments and payments remain accountable human processes.

## 32. Marketing AI

AI may assist with content and campaign analysis under DEC-0059, subject to factual, brand, privacy and legal review.

## 33. Procurement AI

AI may assist with supplier analysis and document review, but purchasing commitments remain governed by approved authority.

## 34. HR AI

AI use in employee workflows must respect privacy, fairness and employment requirements under DEC-0056 and DEC-0058.

## 35. Security AI

AI may assist with anomaly detection and security analysis, with appropriate controls against false positives and unauthorized action.

## 36. Analytics AI

AI-supported analytics must distinguish facts, forecasts, assumptions and recommendations.

## 37. Decision Support

AI should generally improve human decisions rather than obscure who is accountable for them.

## 38. Agent Permissions

Permissions should be scoped by role, task, environment and risk level.

## 39. Secrets Management

Credentials, tokens and keys must not be embedded insecurely in prompts, source code or uncontrolled automation.

## 40. Environment Separation

Development, testing and production automation should be separated where practical.

## 41. Testing

Material automations should be tested against normal, edge and failure cases before production use.

## 42. Change Management

Material AI workflows should follow controlled change procedures with owners and rollback plans.

## 43. Incident Management

AI failures and automation incidents with material impact should enter the relevant incident-management process.

## 44. Bias and Fairness

High-impact AI uses should be assessed for inappropriate bias and unequal outcomes.

## 45. Human Override

Authorized personnel must be able to override or stop material automated behavior.

## 46. Cost Governance

AI usage costs should be monitored and managed against business value.

## 47. Reliability

Critical workflows should not depend on a single AI provider or fragile automation path without appropriate contingency planning.

## 48. Business Continuity

Important AI-assisted processes should have manual fallback procedures.

## 49. AI Metrics

Management should track measures such as accuracy, adoption, time saved, error reduction, cost and business impact.

## 50. Productivity Claims

Reported AI productivity improvements should be measured against actual outcomes rather than activity alone.

## 51. Automation ROI

Automation should be evaluated on total cost, maintenance, reliability and measurable benefit.

## 52. AI Governance Review

Material AI systems should receive periodic governance review based on risk and impact.

## 53. AI Register

The AI register should capture purpose, owner, provider, data, permissions, risk class, status and review date.

## 54. Risk Classification

AI uses should be classified proportionately according to customer, employee, financial, legal, security and operational impact.

## 55. High-Risk Review

Higher-risk AI applications require stronger testing, approvals, monitoring and human oversight.

## 56. Low-Risk Automation

Low-risk repetitive workflows may use lighter controls while retaining ownership and basic monitoring.

## 57. Documentation

Material AI workflows should document intended behavior, limitations and escalation conditions.

## 58. Prompt Libraries

Approved reusable prompts may be maintained for common workflows, with version control where useful.

## 59. Knowledge Sources

AI assistants should preferentially use authoritative internal sources for company-specific answers.

## 60. Source Citations

Where practical, internal AI outputs should identify the source material used for important claims.

## 61. Continuous Learning

AI workflows should be improved based on verified errors, user feedback and measured outcomes.

## 62. Governance Escalation

Material AI risks should be escalated through enterprise risk management under DEC-0051.

## 63. Legal Coordination

AI practices should align with legal and regulatory requirements under DEC-0058.

## 64. Security Coordination

AI infrastructure should align with DEC-0062.

## 65. Data Coordination

AI analytics should align with DEC-0060.

## 66. Technology Coordination

AI platforms should align with DEC-0061.

## 67. Customer Experience

AI must improve customer experience without making it unnecessarily difficult to reach a human when human support is needed.

## 68. Employee Experience

Automation should remove low-value work while preserving employee judgment and appropriate autonomy.

## 69. Automation Ethics

The company should avoid automation whose primary effect is to conceal responsibility, manipulate customers or evade legitimate accountability.

## 70. AI Communications

Public claims about AI capabilities should be accurate and supportable.

## 71. Synthetic Content

AI-generated public content should be reviewed for factual accuracy, authenticity and brand suitability.

## 72. Deepfake and Impersonation Risk

AI-generated media should not be used to impersonate people or create deceptive representations.

## 73. External AI Access

Employees should use approved AI services for company work and follow security guidance.

## 74. Shadow AI

Unapproved AI use involving company data should be discouraged and addressed through training, policy and technical controls.

## 75. AI Procurement

New AI tools should pass appropriate commercial, security, privacy and legal review before material adoption.

## 76. Exit Planning

Material AI dependencies should have documented alternatives or exit considerations.

## 77. Operational Resilience

Critical workflows should avoid single points of failure created by automation.

## 78. Automation Ownership Transfer

When responsibility changes, workflow ownership and access should be updated promptly.

## 79. Retirement

Unused or unsafe automations should be disabled and retired rather than left operating indefinitely.

## 80. AI Inventory Review

The AI register should be reviewed periodically to identify obsolete, duplicate or unmanaged systems.

## 81. Decision Quality

The ultimate measure of AI is whether it improves decision quality and business outcomes.

## 82. Customer Trust

AI adoption should strengthen rather than weaken customer trust.

## 83. Employee Trust

Automation should be implemented transparently enough for employees to understand its purpose and boundaries.

## 84. Governance Dashboard

Management should eventually monitor AI adoption, risk, incidents, cost, value and review status.

## 85. AI Experimentation

New AI capabilities should begin with bounded experiments before broad deployment when uncertainty is high.

## 86. Pilot Controls

Pilots should define scope, success criteria, data boundaries, owners and stop conditions.

## 87. Scaling Criteria

Successful pilots should scale only when reliability, economics, security and operational readiness are demonstrated.

## 88. Human Skills

AI adoption should be paired with development of human skills in judgment, communication, problem-solving and domain expertise.

## 89. No Blind Automation

The company will not automate a process merely because automation is technically possible.

## 90. No Blind AI Adoption

The company will not adopt AI merely because it is fashionable; demonstrated usefulness is required.

## 91. Management Reporting

AI and automation performance should be included in management reporting where material.

## 92. Auditability

Material automated decisions and actions should be sufficiently traceable for review.

## 93. Regulatory Readiness

The company should adapt AI governance as applicable laws, regulations and standards evolve.

## 94. Security Testing

Material AI-enabled systems should receive appropriate security testing, including prompt-injection and data-exposure testing where relevant.

## 95. Red-Team Testing

Higher-risk AI workflows may require adversarial testing before production deployment.

## 96. Failure Learning

AI incidents should produce documented lessons and corrective actions.

## 97. Portfolio Management

Management should periodically compare AI initiatives and discontinue low-value projects.

## 98. Continuous Improvement

AI governance should evolve as capabilities, risks and business requirements change.

## 99. Key Risks

Major AI risks include:

- Hallucination
- Data leakage
- Excessive permissions
- Automation failure
- Bias
- Vendor dependency
- Uncontrolled cost
- Shadow AI
- Poor accountability
- Regulatory non-compliance

## 100. Success Standard

The AI and automation system is successful when leadership can answer:

- What AI do we use?
- Why do we use it?
- Who owns it?
- What data can it access?
- What can it do automatically?
- Where is human approval required?
- What happens when it fails?
- Is it actually improving the business?

## Implementation Sequence

1. Establish the AI inventory, ownership and risk classification.
2. Define approved AI tools and data boundaries.
3. Standardize automation documentation and approval gates.
4. Build controlled internal AI knowledge and productivity workflows.
5. Introduce bounded operational agents and automation pilots.
6. Establish monitoring, incident handling and human-override controls.
7. Measure AI cost, reliability, adoption and business value.
8. Expand only successful and governable use cases.
9. Continuously review security, privacy, legal and regulatory requirements.

## Implementation Status

This decision establishes the **AI governance, automation and intelligent-operations framework**. Specific AI systems and automations should be deployed only through appropriate technology, security, privacy, legal and business-owner controls.
