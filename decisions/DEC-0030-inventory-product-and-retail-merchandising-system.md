## Decision

KAMLESH & SONS will treat **product information, inventory accuracy, and merchandising quality as one connected retail system**.

The objective is to ensure that customers see the right products, the right information, the right availability, and the right experience while employees maintain reliable operational control.

> **The customer should see confidence. The company should have control.**

## 1. Product Information System

Every sellable product should eventually have a structured product record containing appropriate information such as:

- Brand
- Product name
- Category
- Model
- Variant
- SKU
- Key specifications
- Compatibility
- Price
- Warranty information
- Availability
- Relevant accessories
- Product status

Product data should have a defined owner and authoritative source.

## 2. SKU and Product Identity

KAMLESH & SONS should establish consistent identifiers for products and variants.

Where applicable, operational records may also require serial numbers or IMEI information.

Identifiers should be unique, traceable, and consistent across relevant systems.

## 3. Inventory Accuracy

Inventory should be treated as a controlled business asset, not merely a number displayed by software.

The company should maintain processes for:

- Receiving
- Verification
- Storage
- Display allocation
- Transfers
- Reservations
- Sales deduction
- Returns
- Damaged stock
- Missing stock
- Cycle counts
- Reconciliation

Material discrepancies should be investigated and documented.

## 4. Inventory States

Future systems should distinguish relevant inventory states, such as:

- Available
- Reserved
- Display/demo
- Sold
- Awaiting transfer
- Damaged
- Under service
- Returned
- Quarantined

Exact state definitions should be established before implementation.

## 5. Serialised Products

For products requiring serial-level tracking, the system should maintain appropriate links between:

**Product → Variant → SKU → Serial/IMEI → Sale → Customer → Warranty/Service**

Access to sensitive identifiers should be appropriately controlled.

## 6. Receiving

Receiving procedures should verify that delivered products match expected quantities and specifications.

Checks may include:

- Supplier
- Purchase documentation
- Product identity
- Quantity
- Variant
- Serial/IMEI where applicable
- Physical condition
- Packaging
- Accessories

Discrepancies should be recorded before inventory is treated as fully available.

## 7. Stock Transfers

Future multi-store operations will require controlled stock transfers.

Transfers should identify:

- Origin store
- Destination store
- Products/SKUs
- Quantities
- Serial numbers where applicable
- Responsible personnel
- Dispatch time
- Receipt time
- Exceptions

A transfer should not disappear from inventory simply because it is physically moving between locations.

## 8. Inventory Reconciliation

The company should use periodic reconciliation to compare system records with physical reality.

The process should distinguish between:

- Data-entry errors
- Timing differences
- Damaged goods
- Unrecorded movements
- Loss/shrinkage
- System problems

Adjustments should have appropriate authorization and an audit trail.

## 9. Merchandising Philosophy

Merchandising should support the premium store philosophy established in DEC-0028.

Displays should be:

- Minimal
- Clean
- Organized
- Accessible
- Informative
- Interactive
- Current

The goal is not maximum product density.

The goal is **maximum useful product discovery with minimum visual clutter**.

## 10. Live Product Experience

Where commercially and operationally appropriate, priority products should be available for customer interaction.

Demo units should be:

- Functional
- Charged
- Clean
- Updated where appropriate
- Securely managed
- Ready for demonstration

A broken or misleading demo is both an operational failure and a customer-experience failure.

## 11. Product Zoning

Future store design should use logical product zones that help customers understand categories and compare products.

Potential zones may include:

- Smartphones
- Laptops
- Tablets
- Wearables
- Accessories
- Smart Home
- Services

The final zoning model should be validated against store size, assortment, customer behavior, and brand requirements.

## 12. Product Comparison

Merchandising should make meaningful differences easier to understand.

Where appropriate, displays may communicate:

- Key specifications
- Use cases
- Compatibility
- Price
- Financing options
- Warranty/service information

Information should be concise and accurate rather than overwhelming customers with technical specifications.

## 13. Pricing Integrity

Displayed and quoted prices should follow approved pricing controls.

Where prices change, customer-facing information should be updated promptly.

Pricing, discounts, EMI terms, and promotional claims should not be invented or presented ambiguously.

## 14. Promotions

Promotional merchandising should remain consistent with the premium brand position.

Promotions should communicate genuine value without turning the store into a discount-driven environment.

Every promotion should have:

- Defined offer
- Validity period
- Eligibility
- Terms
- Approval
- Responsible owner

## 15. Accessories and Attachments

Accessories should be presented as part of the customer's technology ecosystem rather than as random add-on products.

Relevant recommendations may include:

- Cases
- Screen protection
- Chargers
- Cables
- Audio products
- Storage
- Power accessories
- Compatibility-specific products

Recommendations should be useful and compatible, not purely commission-driven.

## 16. Cross-Selling Philosophy

The company should distinguish between:

**Helpful recommendation** — solves a genuine customer need.

**Unnecessary upsell** — increases the bill without meaningful customer value.

KAMLESH & SONS should prioritize the former.

## 17. Inventory + Customer Experience

Inventory accuracy directly affects trust.

Customers should not be promised a product merely because a system shows an unverified quantity.

Where availability is uncertain, employees should communicate uncertainty honestly and verify before making commitments.

## 18. Inventory + Customer Portal

As established by DEC-0025, the future Customer Portal may display appropriate product, purchase, warranty, and service information.

Customer-facing availability should be based on controlled inventory data and should account for reservations, transfers, and other relevant states.

## 19. Inventory + AI

Customer AI may use approved product and availability data to answer questions.

Internal AI may assist employees with inventory queries where authorized.

AI must not bypass inventory controls or invent availability when authoritative data is unavailable.

## 20. Inventory Security

High-value technology products require appropriate controls against:

- Theft
- Loss
- Unauthorized movement
- Counterfeit substitution
- Serial/IMEI mismatch
- Unauthorized access

Controls should balance security with the open, premium experience established by the brand.

## 21. Supplier and Authenticity Controls

The company should establish processes to verify products received from suppliers and partners.

Where relevant, controls should address:

- Authorized sourcing
- Product authenticity
- Documentation
- Warranty eligibility
- Serial/IMEI consistency
- Damage
- Returns

Specific supplier qualification standards require separate operational and commercial documentation.

## 22. Reporting and KPIs

Future inventory and merchandising dashboards may monitor:

- Inventory accuracy
- Stock turnover
- Sell-through
- Days of inventory
- Stockouts
- Excess stock
- Shrinkage
- Demo availability
- Product-level sales
- Accessory attachment rate
- Gross margin by category

Metrics should support decisions rather than encourage manipulation.

## 23. New Product Launches

Product launches should have a coordinated process covering, where relevant:

- Product data
- Pricing
- Inventory
- Display
- Demo preparation
- Staff training
- Marketing assets
- Customer AI knowledge
- Website/portal information

No launch should rely on one person remembering every dependency.

## 24. Product Retirement

When products are discontinued or no longer strategically supported, the company should manage:

- Remaining stock
- Pricing
- Display removal
- Customer communication where relevant
- Warranty/service implications
- Website/catalog status
- Replacement products

## 25. Multi-Store Scalability

The inventory model should eventually support multiple locations without losing visibility of where products actually are.

The architecture should distinguish:

**Company inventory → Location inventory → Product/SKU → Serial/IMEI where applicable**

Transfers between locations should remain traceable.

## 26. Risks

Major risks include:

- Incorrect stock records
- Selling unavailable products
- Counterfeit or unauthorized products
- Serial/IMEI mismatches
- Excess inventory
- Stockouts
- Shrinkage
- Outdated product information
- Cluttered merchandising
- Broken demo units
- Misleading promotions
- Over-aggressive accessory selling

These risks should be addressed through process, systems, training, controls, and regular review.

## Implementation Sequence

The preferred sequence is:

1. Define product and SKU taxonomy.
2. Define inventory states and ownership.
3. Establish receiving and reconciliation procedures.
4. Build the product information standard.
5. Define merchandising principles.
6. Create priority inventory SOPs.
7. Establish demo-device standards.
8. Introduce inventory reporting.
9. Test processes in the flagship store.
10. Refine for multi-store scalability.

## Implementation Status

This decision establishes the **inventory, product information, and merchandising direction**. It does not select an inventory/POS platform, supplier, barcode system, ERP, or specific inventory KPI targets.

Those decisions require separate requirements, research, operational testing, and approval.

## Review / Supersession

This decision remains active until formally superseded. Inventory and merchandising standards should evolve with assortment, store size, supplier relationships, customer behavior, and expansion.

Material changes should document the rationale, operational impact, affected systems, and related repository documents.
