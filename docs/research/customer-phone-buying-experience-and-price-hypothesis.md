## Research question

Where do customers in the Nepalgunj/Banke catchment experience material difficulty before, during and after buying a phone or laptop, and which combinations of price, channel proof, advice, setup, financing and ownership support change their stated and actual choices?

This study is the canonical customer field instrument for the flagship. It expands the earlier journey hypothesis without accepting a price premium, service bundle or customer-experience claim as policy.

## Evidence labels

- **Verified fact (V):** supported by an identified primary/official source or directly collected and auditable field evidence.
- **Public claim (P):** an interested party's advertised offer; it confirms the claim, not delivery quality.
- **Secondary signal (S):** useful contextual evidence that is not the preferred primary source.
- **Estimate (E):** a transparent calculation from stated inputs.
- **Hypothesis (H):** a proposition requiring local customer, behavioral, commercial or operational evidence.
- **Founder decision (D):** an accepted choice in `decisions/`; this study cannot create one by implication.

## Executive findings

1. **V — Existing Nepal studies disagree on the relative effects of price, features, brand and social influence.** They used Kathmandu Valley or Banepa samples and mostly convenience/judgmental recruitment. They provide a defensible attribute list, not Nepalgunj prevalence, market share or willingness-to-pay estimates.
2. **V — Survey wording, question order and recruitment method can materially change answers.** New questions require cognitive interviews/pretesting; open-ended experience questions should precede prompted lists; unordered options should be randomized; recruitment and participation flow must be disclosed.
3. **V — A stated-choice exercise requires deliberate attributes, levels, experimental design, collection and analysis.** Merely asking “how much extra would you pay?” is not a reliable price-policy basis.
4. **H — K&S can reduce meaningful friction by combining needs-led comparison, documented channel/warranty proof, consent-based migration and case ownership after purchase.** Competitors advertise many components separately; customer value from reliable integration remains unverified.
5. **H — Acceptable price gaps differ by device value, budget, urgency, channel proof, financing and service package.** The earlier 0–3% test was too narrow to locate switching thresholds, so this pilot tests exact NPR differences equivalent to 0%, 1%, 3%, 5%, 8% and 12%.
6. **Method boundary — The planned 100-person purposive sample is directional.** It can reveal mechanisms, compare deliberately covered segments and improve pilots. It cannot produce population market shares, a Nepalgunj margin of error or a bankable single “willingness to pay” number.

## What prior Nepal research can and cannot establish

| Study | Sample/method reported | Finding useful for instrument design | Limitation for K&S |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rai (2021) | 294 smartphone users in Kathmandu Valley; convenience sampling; seven-point scales and SEM | Product attributes and price significantly influenced purchase intention; brand personality did not | Stated intention, one geography and non-probability sample; not local choice behavior or price premium |
| Rai, Dahal & Ghimire (2022) | 394 Kathmandu Valley respondents; convenience sampling | Brand image, product attributes and social factors were reported as significant | Does not establish Banke prevalence or how a retail service bundle changes choice |
| Rai, Dahal & Ghimire (2023) | 398 Kathmandu Valley consumers; convenience sampling | Product price, social factors and brand image were significant; product attributes were not | Conflicts with other Nepal work and remains geographically/non-probability limited |
| Vaidya & Gelal (2025) | 235 Banepa smartphone users; the paper describes convenience sampling despite also using “random” wording | Physical dimensions, reviews/recommendations and features were important; market attributes including price/place/promotion were not significant | Method wording is ambiguous; Banepa results cannot be treated as Nepalgunj demand or WTP |
| Humagain (2022) | Kathmandu Valley smartphone-purchase study | Adds after-sales service, price, promotion, mobile attributes and social influence to the candidate set | Use as construct discovery until exact effect estimates and local relevance are verified |
| Sthapit et al. (2018) | 340 graduate students in Kathmandu; judgmental sampling | Compatibility, social influence and features were significant; price and quality were not | Older student-only, non-probability evidence; contextual only |

**Interpretation:** the apparent contradictions are a research signal, not a choice between papers. K&S should measure actual recent behavior first, then unprompted friction, then controlled stated choices. None of these papers validates a K&S premium or a flagship service package.

## Buying journey and candidate friction map

The initial journey is retained as a hypothesis: need recognition → research → shortlist → price/channel comparison → physical validation → trust validation → affordability → purchase/setup → ownership support.

The field study must ask about **experienced events**, not just whether a broad feature sounds important.

| Stage | Candidate friction to test | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Before buying | Unclear fit; specification overload; conflicting reviews/advice; undisclosed price/stock; authenticity/warranty uncertainty; no useful demo; unclear EMI/trade-in eligibility; sales pressure | Abandoned/restarted search, stores/channels checked, time spent, wrong shortlist, unplanned travel |
| During buying | Final price or gift changes; model/colour/condition mismatch; invoice/MDMS/warranty confusion; finance decline or changed terms; rushed setup; unsafe data handling; incompatible accessory; unclear return/DOA terms | Quote/invoice, declined application, additional payment, missing document, setup time, immediate complaint |
| After buying | DOA/return dispute; warranty handoff; no repair updates; data-privacy concern; unexpected parts/transport cost; long downtime; repeat failure; weak resale/trade-in support; unwanted marketing | Case record, days without device, direct cost, travel, contacts, resolution, repeat purchase intent |

## Hypotheses under test

| ID | Hypothesis | What would support it | What would weaken it |
|---|---|---|---|
| CX-H01 | Decision uncertainty is a material local friction | Repeated unprompted examples with high severity/time cost and preference for needs-led comparison in choice tasks | Rare/low-severity mentions and no choice effect |
| CX-H02 | Documented official-channel/warranty proof changes choice | Customers switch toward a documented offer at a measurable price gap; recent cases show avoided cost/risk | Proof is ignored even at equal price or customers cannot distinguish it after explanation |
| CX-H03 | Consent-based setup/migration adds value | Buyers report real setup/privacy burden and choose the service when trade-offs are explicit | Service is rarely used, easily self-solved or creates more privacy concern than value |
| CX-H04 | Retailer-owned warranty/repair coordination is differentiating | High unresolved burden plus choice/adoption lift and workable partner SLA/economics | Existing authorized service is already easy or case ownership cost/liability overwhelms value |
| CX-H05 | Transparent eligible EMI affects conversion | Finance need appears in actual abandoned/delayed purchases and verified pilot approvals convert | High stated interest but low eligibility/use or margin-destructive partner terms |
| CX-H06 | Acceptable price gap varies by segment and service package | Monotonic switching patterns differ by budget/channel/urgency with adequate cell counts | Inconsistent answers, dominant price-only choice or no repeatable segment pattern |

## Study architecture

### Phase A — twelve discovery and cognitive interviews

Complete before the main instrument is locked. These participants do not count toward the 100 valid main interviews.

- Recruit buyers or co-decision-makers aged 18+ who bought in the last 24 months or expect to buy within 12 months.
- Cover phones and laptops, multiple budgets, women and men, different occupations, Nepalgunj/Kohalpur and frequent border shoppers.
- Ask participants to think aloud: what each question means, how they recall price/time, whether Nepali/English retail terms are understood and why each choice feels realistic.
- Start with open narration before showing the friction list.
- Revise unclear, leading, double-barrelled or culturally awkward wording; record every revision and reason.
- Test the visual choice cards and exact NPR arithmetic. A respondent should not need to calculate percentages.

Store every approach/disposition, respondent, friction event and choice task in the canonical collection workbook under one non-identifying respondent ID. Freeze the approved source and reviewed Nepali versions in its Version Register before production; do not store contact details in the workbook or GitHub.

### Phase B — 100 valid directional customer interviews

The quotas create analytic coverage; they are **not estimates of population composition**.

| Primary intent stratum | Valid interviews |
|---|---:|
| Phone below NPR 25,000 | 20 |
| Phone NPR 25,000–50,000 | 25 |
| Phone NPR 50,000–100,000 | 20 |
| Phone above NPR 100,000 | 15 |
| Laptop below NPR 80,000 | 8 |
| Laptop NPR 80,000–130,000 | 8 |
| Laptop above NPR 130,000 | 4 |
| **Total** | **100** |

Coverage overlays:

- at least 30 must have completed a phone/laptop purchase in the prior 18 months;
- at least 20 must have seriously compared an India-border or parallel/grey option;
- record Nepalgunj/Kohalpur/other Banke residence and frequency of Rupaidiha shopping separately;
- aim for diversity by age, gender and occupation, but do not retrospectively describe an unverified quota as representative.

Recruitment controls:

- at least 50 neutral intercept interviews across varied places, days and times—not only outside technology stores;
- no more than 25 combined from K&S social audiences, waitlists or followers;
- up to 25 from community, workplace, campus and referral routes, excluding K&S staff, immediate founder family and suppliers;
- record approach, eligible, consented, completed and invalid counts by channel;
- do not make a discount, stock allocation, EMI approval, job or service conditional on participation;
- incentives, if any, require founder approval and must be disclosed.

### Phase C — ten documented ownership/service cases

Sample authorized Nepal, lawful personal-import and seller-warranty devices where evidence exists. With consent, capture fault, diagnosis, approval/denial, travel/shipping, parts, direct cost, days without device, updates, data handling, repeat failure and final outcome. This is mechanism evidence, not a channel failure rate.

### Phase D — behavioral validation

After an authorized pop-up or store pilot exists, connect stated results to:

- actual quote-to-purchase conversion by offer and price gap;
- lost-opportunity reasons under DEC-0048;
- actual setup, finance and service uptake;
- contribution after partner, labor, subsidy and failure cost;
- repeat use, resolution and complaint outcomes.

No permanent pricing or service promise should rely on stated-choice data alone.

## Canonical questionnaire

Administer in this order to reduce prompting. Keep identity/contact data separate from answers under DEC-0054.

### S — consent and eligibility

| ID | Field | Response/control |
|---|---|---|
| S01 | Age | Terminate if under 18 |
| S02 | Catchment relationship | Resident, worker/student, frequent shopper, other; record place |
| S03 | Decision role | Primary or shared decision-maker required |
| S04 | Device timing | Bought within 24 months and/or likely purchase within 12 months |
| S05 | Study consent | Voluntary; purpose, duration, privacy and withdrawal explained |
| S06 | Evidence/contact consent | Separate optional permissions; never make contact permission mandatory |

### B — recent and intended behavior

Ask about the latest phone/laptop purchase first, then the next intended purchase.

| ID | Field | Response/control |
|---|---|---|
| B01 | Exact recent device | Brand, model, RAM/storage/configuration; “do not know” allowed |
| B02 | Purchase timing/place/channel | Month/year, place, seller type; analyst classifies channel from evidence |
| B03 | Amount/payment | Final amount, cash/card/EMI/mixed, down payment/tenure where applicable |
| B04 | Alternatives | Exact models/channels and executable quoted amounts where recalled/evidenced |
| B05 | Proof checked | Invoice, IMEI/MDMS, seal/activation, warranty, seller identity, none/don't know |
| B06 | Search effort | Days/weeks, number of stores/sites, travel and main information sources |
| B07 | Intended device/job | Phone/laptop and primary jobs: communication, study, work, business, camera, gaming/creation, other |
| B08 | Intended budget | Exact range; enumerator maps to quota after answer |
| B09 | Timing/urgency | Expected month and whether replacement can wait |

### O — unprompted experience

Ask before any prompted list.

1. **O01:** Tell me how you chose the last device, from first thought to after purchase.
2. **O02:** What was the hardest or most confusing part?
3. **O03:** What did you worry might go wrong?
4. **O04:** What actually went wrong, if anything?
5. **O05:** If you could change one thing about buying or owning that device, what would it be?

Record words as spoken, then code later. Do not translate an answer into a K&S feature during the interview.

### F — prompted friction event loop

Randomize unordered items within each stage. For every item, ask whether it happened on the recent purchase/service episode: **yes / no / not applicable / cannot recall**. If yes, collect:

| ID suffix | Measure | Response |
|---|---|---|
| `_SEV` | Severity | 0 “no burden” to 10 “extreme burden” |
| `_TIME` | Extra time | Minutes/hours/days, with unit |
| `_COST` | Direct extra cost | NPR; zero allowed; do not force an estimate |
| `_RES` | Resolution | Fully, partly, not resolved, still open |
| `_WHO` | Expected responsible party | Seller, brand/distributor, service center, bank/finance, self, other |
| `_TRUST` | Effect on choosing same seller/channel again | More likely, no change, less likely, definitely not |

Candidate event codes:

- `F_BEF_FIT`, `F_BEF_INFO`, `F_BEF_PRICE`, `F_BEF_STOCK`, `F_BEF_AUTH`, `F_BEF_DEMO`, `F_BEF_FIN`, `F_BEF_PRESSURE`;
- `F_DUR_PRICE`, `F_DUR_VARIANT`, `F_DUR_DOCS`, `F_DUR_FIN`, `F_DUR_SETUP`, `F_DUR_PRIVACY`, `F_DUR_ACCESSORY`, `F_DUR_DOAINFO`;
- `F_AFT_DOA`, `F_AFT_HANDOFF`, `F_AFT_UPDATES`, `F_AFT_PRIVACY`, `F_AFT_COST`, `F_AFT_DOWNTIME`, `F_AFT_REPEAT`, `F_AFT_RESALE`, `F_AFT_MARKETING`.

Add “other” at every stage. The discovery interviews may add or remove event codes before field lock.

### P — attribute priority

Do not begin with a long importance-rating grid where everything can become “very important.”

1. Ask the participant's **top three** purchase/ownership factors in their own words.
2. Then show a randomized set of candidate attributes and ask which is **most important** and **least important** in each balanced four-item task.
3. Use six short tasks only if the cognitive test confirms comprehension; otherwise retain the top-three forced choice.

Candidate attributes: final price, exact stock, product fit/advice, physical trial, channel/authenticity proof, documented warranty, finance, trade-in, migration/setup, accessory compatibility, retailer case ownership and delivery/pickup.

### C — randomized stated-choice pilot

This is a **pilot**, not a population valuation or approved conjoint model.

Each respondent receives eight two-offer choices plus **“postpone/buy neither.”** Both offers name the same exact reference device/configuration. Use a real same-day reference price the respondent could plausibly act on. Show the price difference in both NPR and percent; do not require arithmetic.

| Attribute | Levels available for experimental design |
|---|---|
| Final price difference from reference | 0%, 1%, 3%, 5%, 8%, 12% converted to exact NPR |
| Channel/warranty proof | Seller statement/receipt; documented Nepal VAT invoice + authorized Nepal warranty |
| Advice/demo | Standard sales interaction; needs-led shortlist + comparable live trial |
| Setup/privacy | Self setup; consent checklist + migration/privacy closeout |
| Ownership support | Customer contacts brand/service center; retailer owns intake, updates and handoff to documented resolution |
| Finance | Cash/card only; transparent EMI **only if respondent is eligible for the described route** |

Design controls:

- construct a balanced, blocked design before launch; do not invent combinations during interviews;
- use eight blocks and assign them randomly, targeting at least ten valid respondents per block;
- vary offer position and avoid implausible or internally contradictory profiles;
- include one obvious dominance/comprehension task that is excluded from preference estimation;
- randomize task order within block;
- ask choice, confidence (0–10) and short reason after each task;
- never describe undocumented supply as “fake” or imply an illegal act;
- if a finance route is not confirmed or the respondent is ineligible, show no finance attribute rather than an impossible promise;
- retain the complete design table, block/version, assignment and reference-price evidence with the dataset.

An experienced choice-model analyst should review the design before production. The ISPOR task-force guidance is used here for process discipline—research question, attributes/levels, experimental design, elicitation, instrument, collection, analysis and reporting—not as evidence about the Nepal retail market.

### R — recognition and follow-up

Only after behavior and choices:

- `R01`: Which tested offer components would the respondent actually use in the next purchase?
- `R02`: What proof or service standard would make the component credible?
- `R03`: What would make the respondent distrust or reject it?
- `R04`: Optional separate consent for a later real-offer pilot; store contact data separately.

## Field and data-quality protocol

### Enumerator rules

- Read neutral text consistently; do not sell K&S, defend official channels or explain which answer is preferred.
- Record “do not know,” refusal and missing separately from “no” and zero.
- Use Nepali or English according to participant choice; retain the approved translation/version.
- Never ask to see private device content, passwords, bank credentials or unnecessary identity data.
- Evidence viewing is optional; redact personal identifiers and record evidence type, not sensitive numbers, unless a separate approved protocol requires them.
- Stop if a participant withdraws. Do not link answers to marketing without separate opt-in.

### Minimum validity checks

Mark a record invalid before analysis when it is duplicate, ineligible, lacks consent, is substantially incomplete, is demonstrably fabricated, or was completed under material interviewer deviation. Flag—rather than automatically delete—very short duration, repetitive patterns, inconsistent timing/price or failure of the dominance check. Two failed comprehension checks require review and normally exclusion from choice analysis.

Maintain a disposition log: approached, screened, eligible, refused, partial, complete, invalid and valid by recruitment channel. If more than 15% of completes fail quality review, pause and investigate before replacement interviews; the threshold is a project control, not a statistical theorem.

## Analysis and reporting plan

### Descriptive friction evidence

- Report counts and within-sample percentages with denominators and missing/not-applicable values.
- Separate spontaneous mentions from prompted events.
- For each event report occurrence, median severity, time/cost distributions, unresolved count and trust effect; do not collapse unlike burdens into an unexplained score.
- Cross-tab only where cells are large enough to display responsibly: device, budget stratum, recent/intended buyer, documented channel class and geography.
- Report recent-purchase cases separately from future-buyer opinions.

### Choice evidence

- First report raw choice shares by exact price gap and service/proof package, with respondent counts and opt-out rates.
- Check whether choice generally declines as the K&S-equivalent price gap rises; investigate non-monotonic or dominance failures.
- If a qualified analyst estimates a conditional/mixed logit model, account for repeated choices within respondent, document coding and model specification, and label results exploratory.
- Report **within-sample switching ranges**, not a single population WTP value.
- Do not attach a Nepalgunj population confidence interval or margin of error to this purposive sample. Model standard errors do not repair recruitment bias.

### Decision evidence table

No feature progresses because it was frequently selected in a survey. Build one row per proposed feature:

| Evidence dimension | Required question |
|---|---|
| Experienced need | How often and how severely did this happen in the directional sample? |
| Choice effect | Did the feature change controlled choices, and at what gaps/segments? |
| Actual use | Did customers adopt it in an executable pilot? |
| Commercial effect | What happened to conversion, contribution, labor and failure cost? |
| Operational proof | Can K&S and partners deliver the promise consistently and safely? |
| Strategic role | Table stakes, experiment, differentiator candidate or reject? |
| Decision | Which founder/owner approval is required before scale? |

## Evidence gates before interpretation

The desk instrument is complete; the study is not.

- 12 discovery/cognitive interviews completed and revisions logged;
- choice design reviewed and frozen with version-controlled blocks;
- 100 valid main interviews meeting the stated strata and recruitment controls;
- at least ten valid assignments per choice block;
- 30+ recent-purchase participants and 20+ serious border/parallel comparers;
- 10 documented service cases;
- disposition, missingness, exclusions, dates, locations and interviewer IDs reported;
- anonymized dataset/codebook and reproducible analysis retained internally;
- actual-behavior pilot completed before permanent price/service policy.

If recruitment deviates, report the deviation and its effect; do not silently refill convenient channels and preserve the word “balanced.” A larger probability-oriented or carefully calibrated study is required before population prevalence or market-share claims.

## Interpretation boundaries

This work does **not** establish that:

- customers will pay more for “premium experience”;
- 0–3% or any other price gap is acceptable;
- official Nepal supply always has the lowest ownership cost;
- every advertised service should be launched;
- online followers represent the Banke customer population;
- stated intention equals conversion;
- the flagship is commercially viable.

## Batch synthesis

### What we learned

- Nepal literature provides a broad candidate set—price, attributes, brand/social influence, reviews and after-sales—but offers conflicting results and no reliable Nepalgunj WTP estimate.
- Customer research must separate actual experienced friction, prompted recognition, stated trade-offs and real purchasing behavior.
- The previously planned social/waitlist-heavy funnel would over-represent people already engaged with K&S.

### What changed in assumptions

- The old 0–3% price-gap ladder becomes one narrow region inside a 0–12% stated-choice pilot; it is not a proposed pricing rule.
- “100 customers” is now a controlled directional sample with phone/laptop strata, recruitment limits and disclosure rules—not a representative market survey.
- Direct willingness-to-pay questions are secondary; controlled choices and subsequent real-offer behavior carry more weight.

### K&S opportunities

- Test an integrated confidence system: needs-led comparison, documentary proof, consent-based migration and retailer-owned case updates.
- Use experienced time, direct cost and unresolved outcomes to design services around real burden rather than aesthetic “premium” language.
- Connect research directly to lost-sale, contribution and delivery evidence before scale.

### Risks

- Hypothetical and social-desirability bias can overstate demand for responsible service.
- Non-probability recruitment can hide important segments and cannot estimate population shares.
- Broad service bundles can conceal which element creates value while increasing cost/liability.
- An apparent preference is commercially useless if partner terms, eligibility or execution fail.

### Unresolved questions

- Which frictions are most common and costly in Banke, by device and budget?
- Which proof/service attributes change choices after exact price is visible?
- Do stated switching ranges predict real conversion?
- What does each credible service cost to deliver, and which promise can K&S sustain?

### Recommended next research

1. Founder approves the field protocol/budget; complete 12 discovery/cognitive interviews and freeze the instrument.
2. Execute the 100 valid interviews, 10 service cases and linked 30 recent-purchase audits.
3. In the desk sequence, move next to supplier credit, price protection, stock rotation and brand/distributor support; those terms constrain which customer promises are viable.
